# You and Your Research

**Source**: Richard W. Hamming, Bellcore, March 7, 1986. Transcript of the famous talk on doing first-class research.

## Key Points

- **Work on important problems.** If you don't work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work.
- **The compound effect of small daily advantages.** Working slightly harder, thinking slightly more deeply, compounds over a career.
- **Keep your door open (or closed).** Open door = short-term recognition but interrupted deep work. Closed door = fewer distractions but isolation.
- **Great scientists have the courage to pursue their ideas despite criticism.**
- **The prepared mind.** Luck favors the prepared mind — continuously expose yourself to problems and ideas.

## Core Insights

### On Working on Important Problems

> "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work."

Hamming observed that great scientists regularly asked themselves: "What are the important problems in my field? What important problems am I working on? If what I'm doing is not important, why am I working on it?"

He recommended setting aside time every Friday to think about the big picture — not the immediate task, but the direction of your work.

### On Compound Advantage

Hamming noted that great scientists seemed to work only slightly harder than average ones, but the difference compounded. Einstein and others would work on Christmas, on weekends — not frantically, but consistently. The small gap in effort and thinking quality, sustained over decades, produces an enormous gap in output.

### On Courage and Independence

> "Once you get your courage up and believe that you can do important problems, then you can."

Great scientists have the courage to pursue their own ideas when others dismiss them. They also have the ability to change direction when evidence shows they're wrong — a form of intellectual courage.

### On Open vs Closed Doors

Hamming observed a trade-off: scientists with open doors (always available) got more short-term interruptions but also more collaborations and recognition. Those with closed doors got more done but risked isolation. The ideal: close your door when doing deep work, open it when generating ideas.

### On Selling Your Work

> "You have to learn to write clearly and well so that people will read it."

Hamming stressed that great work that isn't communicated effectively might as well not exist. Writing, speaking, and presenting are not secondary skills — they're essential to having your work matter.

## The Question

Hamming would ask colleagues: "What are the most important problems in your field?" Then: "What important problems are you working on?" If the answers didn't match, he'd ask: "Why aren't you working on them?"

This repeated self-interrogation was, in his view, the single biggest factor separating first-rate scientists from the rest.
